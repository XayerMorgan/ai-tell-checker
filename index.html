Help & About

What this tool does

This tool scans pasted text for patterns documented in frequency studies and editorial write-ups as more common in AI-generated writing than in typical human prose. It reports a density score (flags per 1,000 words) and lets you inspect every flagged instance in context.

It does not detect AI authorship. No published method can reliably prove a specific passage was AI-written once a human has reviewed or lightly edited it. This tool is a triage aid, the same way a style linter points at passive voice or long sentences without proving who wrote them.

Scoring rubric & density thresholds

The score normalizes pattern frequency across texts of any length:

Score = (Total Flagged Patterns ÷ Word Count) × 1,000

  • LOW (< 3.0 flags / 1,000 words): Typical of clean, unassisted human prose with natural vocabulary variance, varied sentence cadence, and minimal formulaic transitions.
  • MODERATE (3.0 – 7.9 flags / 1,000 words): Contains noticeable clusters of flagged constructs (such as multiple em dashes, stock transitional phrases, or corporate softening fillers). Common in corporate correspondence, routine administrative drafting, or lightly edited AI copy.
  • HIGH (8.0+ flags / 1,000 words): Heavy saturation of flagged patterns (representing on average at least one flag every 70–120 words). Raw, unedited AI output routinely scores 10.0 to 25.0+ because LLMs systematically replicate repetitive transitional phrases, pervasive em dashes, tricolons, and post-2023 frequency-spike vocabulary.

Why would 14.2 per 1,000 be High? (Declaration of Independence case study)

A prominent test case is the official National Archives transcription of the Declaration of Independence (1,335 words), which scores 14.2 flags / 1,000 words (HIGH). Here is why:

  • 15 Rule-of-three / parallelism hits: Classical 18th-century oratorical and legal drafting heavily relied on rhythmic tricolons and parallel triadic clauses (e.g., "plundered our seas, ravaged our Coasts, burnt our towns", "works of death, desolation and tyranny", "levy War, conclude Peace, contract Alliances").
  • 3 Em dash / double hyphen hits: The National Archives Stone Engraving transcription contains period-typical ASCII double-hyphens (--That to secure..., --That whenever..., --Such has been...) as dramatic pause markers.
  • 1 Lexical overuse hit: The word "Boundaries" ("enlarging its Boundaries so as to render it...").

The lesson: 14.2 is mathematically HIGH because 18th-century high-oratory prose and unedited LLM prose share superficial structural characteristics: intense rhythmic tricolons, dense comma-separated parallelisms, and dramatic punctuation. This illustrates why density scoring is an editorial triage aid pointing editors at dense rhetorical habits, and never a verdict on authorship.

Categories explained

Lexical overuse (banned vocabulary)
Words that showed statistically significant frequency increases in academic writing after ChatGPT's public launch (e.g., "delve," "underscore," "meticulous," "commendable"). Source: PubMed and arXiv word-frequency studies, 2024-2026.
e.g. "We delved into the intricate landscape of..."
Stock AI phrases
Boilerplate transitional phrases common in generated essay- and report-style prose: "in today's fast-paced world," "it's worth noting that," "a testament to."
e.g. "In today's fast-paced world, it's worth noting that..."
Corporate / email AI patterns
Filler and softening phrases specific to AI-generated professional email and memo tone: opening pleasantries, "circle back," "gentle reminder," "as we navigate," "please don't hesitate to reach out." Added after real-world testing showed AI-generated emails often use zero academic-style vocabulary but heavy use of this register instead.
e.g. "I hope this finds you well. As we navigate the coming quarter..."
Em dash / double hyphen density
Counted per 1,000 words. A controlled test found GPT-3.5 produced 0 em dashes across 10 generated stories, while GPT-4o and GPT-4.1 produced 14-16, plausibly because the em dash is a single token in GPT-4-family tokenizers versus 2-3 tokens for a comma-plus-conjunction. Multiple independent sources caution this is not reliable evidence on its own; human formal writers use em dashes heavily too, and OpenAI has since made the habit suppressible via custom instructions. Flagged for density review against your own baseline, not as a standalone signal.
Contrast-framing rhetoric
"It was not X, it was Y" / "She did not just feel it, she became it" constructions, a repetitive dramatic sentence architecture common in AI-generated narrative and marketing copy.
Rule-of-three lists
Three-item comma sequences used as a rhythmic device ("furious, frightened, undone"). This is a heuristic pattern match, not a syntax parser. As of v1.2.0 it excludes runs of Title-Case proper nouns (names, states, delegations) after testing showed signature blocks and place-name lists produced heavy false positives. It will still catch legitimate lowercase/mixed-case three-item lists in ordinary human writing. A single hit is not meaningful; a cluster across a short passage is worth a look.
Reflexive hedging
Softening counterpoints attached to nearly every claim ("however," "that said," "it's important to note"), which can indicate a model avoiding a firm position.
Markdown emphasis markers
Bold/italic markup on statements that may not warrant emphasis. Reflects an observed pattern where models emphasize text indiscriminately because they weight importance differently than a human editor would.

Known limitations

  • Rule-of-three and contrast-framing detection are regex heuristics, not syntactic parsing. Expect some false positives on legitimate three-item lists and negation sentences.
  • The lexical and phrase lists reflect patterns documented as of late 2025 / 2026. Specific words fall in and out of favor as they get publicly flagged, the same drift already documented for "delve" itself, so the list needs periodic revision, not one-time use.
  • This tool only analyzes text. It has no equivalent check for AI-generated images.
  • A low score does not clear a passage, and a high score does not convict one. Always read the flagged context before acting on it.
